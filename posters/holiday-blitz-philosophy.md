# Equatorial Chalk

**A design movement for mathematics under an African sun.**

Equatorial Chalk begins where two surfaces meet: the deep slate of a classroom
blackboard and the enormous, generous light of an equatorial sky. It treats the
blackboard not as furniture but as landscape — a night-dark ground on which
knowledge is cultivated the way terraced fields are cultivated: patiently, in
measured rows, one deliberate mark at a time. Every composition is built from
the instruments of learning itself — the tick of a number line, the arc of a
protractor, the counted dot, the shaded segment — accumulated with the density
of a scientist's field notebook until the marks themselves become terrain,
horizon, and weather. Nothing is decorated; everything is *plotted*.

Color is rationed like water in the dry season. One ground: slate so deep it
reads as night soil. Two living pigments: the emerald of new maize after the
rains, and the hot amber of ten o'clock sun. A whisper of sky-blue for what is
measured, and bone-chalk white for what is written by hand. No gradients that
nature would not produce; light in this movement comes from layered opacity —
rings within rings, each drawn separately, as a master draughtsman would build
luminosity through patient superimposition rather than a filter's shortcut.

Space is organized as the equator organizes a day: a vast sky, a precise
horizon, and worked ground below. The horizon is always a measured line — ticked,
numbered, exact — because in this movement the land itself has been surveyed by
a teacher's hand. Above it, celestial forms are built from mathematics: suns
assembled from concentric counted rings, their rays the radii of an invisible
protractor. Below it, the ground is cut into cultivated strips — the visual
grammar of the fraction bar — each strip shaded to a different measure, so the
fields themselves quietly count. Scale is fearless: one monumental form
commands the sky while a hundred meticulous small marks reward the viewer who
steps closer. The tension between the one and the many is the movement's pulse.

Typography enters as chalk enters a lesson: rarely, confidently, and always as
part of the diagram. A few enormous words set with the vertical compression of
hand-painted Kenyan shop signage — letters as architecture, doing in six words
what a paragraph would spoil. Beneath them, small systematic labels in a
draughtsman's monospace, placed like the annotations of a survey map: quiet
coordinates, dates, codes. Text never floats; it is registered to the grid of
the composition, aligned to tick marks, locked to margins with the obsessive
precision of someone who has redrawn the layout forty times and will not accept
a single misaligned baseline.

The finished artifact must feel labored over — the product of countless hours
of measuring, erasing, and redrawing by someone at the absolute top of their
craft. Faint construction lines may remain, like the ghost grid of graph paper
under ink, precisely because a master leaves evidence of method. Every ring
counted, every tick equidistant, every margin breathing. The work should hang
comfortably in a gallery and yet survive being photographed on a phone, printed
cheaply, and pinned to a school gate in the sun — monumental at ten meters,
meticulous at ten centimeters. That double life, mass-produced yet
painstakingly hand-plotted, is the whole point of Equatorial Chalk.
