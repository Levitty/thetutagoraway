# How questions and examples should be shown: evidence and design rules

Research, 30 Sep 2026. mathacademy.com, justinmath.com and eedi.substack.com were
blocked from the research environment, so Math Academy and Eedi are described
from secondary write-ups. [PK] = the researcher's prior knowledge, not re-checked.

## 1. Worked examples and fading
- Novices learn more from studying worked examples than from unaided problem
  solving; alternate examples with problems (IES practice guide, Pashler 2007).
- Fade steps with self-explanation prompts: better transfer and retention
  (Atkinson, Renkl & Merrill 2003). Adaptive fading beats fixed fading, which
  beats plain problem solving (Salden, Aleven & Renkl 2010).
- Example-then-problem pairs work best during learning (Van Gog et al. 2011).
- Self-explanation helps children aged 8-11 (Rittle-Johnson 2006).
- Explaining an incorrect worked example alongside correct ones improves
  understanding (Booth et al. 2013, "Algebra by Example").
- Expertise reversal: examples stop helping fluent learners (Kalyuga).

**Rules**
1. New skill: one fully worked example with a self-explanation prompt, then a
   matched problem, then a faded example (last step blank), then two steps
   blank, then independent problems.
2. Fade backwards; remove a step only after the child gets it right about
   twice; restore it on an error.
3. Self-explanation prompts as multiple choice, not free text (young and
   second-language learners).
4. From the 2nd-3rd session, mix in "Juma's mistake" examples: a worked
   solution with a known misconception, "where did Juma go wrong?". Never
   incorrect examples alone.
5. Skip examples for children who get the first two problems right cold.
6. Small steps: Math Academy's knowledge points each start with a worked
   example; move on after 2 correct in a row.

## 2. Concrete-pictorial-abstract and the bar model
- Concreteness fading beats staying concrete or starting abstract (Fyfe &
  McNeil 2014). Link the representations explicitly (IES 2007, 2012).
- Singapore bar model: part-whole, comparison, multiplicative; lets children
  solve algebraic word problems before algebra (Ng & Lee 2009). Works for
  fractions, ratio and percentage. Draw bars to scale.

| Problem | How to draw it |
|---|---|
| Part-whole (+/−) | One bar in parts, total braced above, "?" on the unknown |
| Comparison | Two bars left-aligned; the difference is the overhang |
| "3 times as many" | Bar A = 1 unit; bar B = 3 equal units |
| 3/5 of 40 | 5 equal units, 3 shaded, whole = 40; 1 unit = 8 |
| Spent 3/5, KSh 12 left | 5 units; 2 unshaded = 12; 1 unit = 6; whole = 30 |
| Ratio 2:3 | Bars of 2 and 3 equal units; find 1 unit |
| Percentage | A bar of 10 pieces of 10% |

**Rules**
1. On one screen: objects, bar and equation together with colour links, then
   remove the objects, then the bar.
2. Move up a level on performance (about 2-3 right in a row), not on grade;
   drop back one level for the next item after 2 errors or a
   misconception-flagged error.
3. Keep bar models as a hint for ratio, percentage and equations up to
   Grade 9.
4. Vary the concrete materials so meaning doesn't stick to surface features.

## 3. Retrieval, spacing, interleaving, mastery
- Interleaved maths practice: 61% vs 38% a month later (Rohrer et al. 2019,
  54 Grade 7 classes, d = 0.83).
- Successive relearning: 1 correct in each of 3 spaced sessions beats 3 in one.
- Khan: familiar / proficient / mastered, mastered needs a mixed-skill check.
- Math Academy: implicit repetition credits prerequisites (FIRe).

**Rules**
1. Blocked practice only at introduction; after that at least half of a
   session interleaved, never two consecutive items with the same strategy.
2. Interleave confusable pairs on purpose: area vs perimeter, × vs + of
   fractions, ratio vs difference, "increase by" vs "increase to".
3. Mastery in stages: acquired (unaided at abstract level), stable (right on a
   mixed item in a later session), retained (right on spaced reviews at about
   1 day, 1 week, 3-4 weeks); a failed review lowers it.
4. Answers given after a hint are practice, not mastery evidence.

## 4. Feedback after a wrong answer
- Elaborated feedback ES 0.49 vs correct answer 0.32 vs right/wrong 0.05
  (Van der Kleij et al. 2015). Specific, task-focused, short (Shute 2008).
- 14-36% of actions are hint click-through (LearnLab).

**Rules**
1. Wrong answer matches a known misconception: say what they probably did
   (no blame), show why it can't be right with a quick check or picture, give
   the right idea in 1-2 lines, then a NEW item of the same type.
2. Unrecognised error: "Not quite" plus hint 1, one more try.
3. Second wrong: full worked solution, then a near-identical item.
4. Never "try again" with nothing new.
5. Short minimum wait before hints 2 and 3 unlock.
6. Re-check a seen misconception in a later session.
7. Task-focused tone: "The 3 is in the tens place, so..." not "Wrong!".

## 6. What strong programmes do
- **Eedi / Diagnostic Questions:** 4 options, 1 correct, each wrong option tied
  to a misconception; a child holding the misconception must not be able to
  get it right ("unproductive success").
- **Math Academy:** fine knowledge points, worked example first, pass on 2 in a
  row, explanation for every question, knowledge graph, spaced interleaved
  review.
- **Khan:** stepwise hints; hint = not mastery evidence; mastery needs mixed
  practice.
- **Zearn:** "Boosts" at the point of error bring in prior-grade
  representations.
- **Maths No Problem:** anchor task, discussion, guided then independent
  practice, CPA throughout.

**Rules**
1. Multiple-choice wrong options come from misconception generators, never
   random nearby numbers.
2. 3-5 diagnostic questions to open a topic.
3. On an error traced to a prerequisite, a 2-3 item prior-grade mini-sequence
   with its picture, then back.
4. Mastery checks use "prove it" items (0.4 vs 0.35, not 0.4 vs 0.3).

## 7. English as a second language (Kenya)
- English competence correlates strongly with maths word-problem scores in
  Kenyan studies; children do better on bare-number items. English from
  Grade 4, with code-switching in practice (UNICEF 2016).
- Read-aloud helps most on hard-to-read items and for young poor readers.
  Narration + picture beats on-screen text + picture (Mayer).

**Rules**
1. Speaker button on every question and hint (default on in Grades 1-3),
   optional Kiswahili, words highlighted as read.
2. Word problems: sentences of 15 words or fewer, one fact each, present
   tense, active voice, no idioms, question last, familiar Kenyan names and
   contexts.
3. First show a word problem as a picture or bar model with few words, then
   with full words.
4. Per-topic vocabulary bank (sum, difference, product, quotient, perimeter,
   denominator, "more than", "times as many"), tap for picture, audio and
   Kiswahili; pre-teach 3-5 words.
5. Log a language miss (fails the word version, passes the bare number)
   separately from a maths miss; give reading support, not maths remediation.
6. Pair comparison sentences ("5 less than x") with a comparison bar.
7. Short feedback with a picture and audio, next to the working it's about.

## Where the evidence is weaker
Most worked-example and retrieval studies are with older students or adults;
the 2-3-correct CPA threshold is a heuristic; DreamBox and Zearn efficacy is
mostly vendor-reported; Kenyan evidence is mainly correlational.
