# HOREB exemplar stretches, round 3 (DRAFT, not loaded anywhere)

Same format as EXEMPLARS.md and the round 2 drafts; numbering continues from 10. Moves are in [brackets] and use PLAYBOOK.md names, the round 2 moves (17 to 24) and the round 3 moves (R3-1 to R3-10, PLAYBOOK_ROUND3.md). Board actions are in (brackets). The child's name is Wanjiru. Each stretch ends with a bad turn and the good turn that replaces it.

These cover the hard moments: a silent child, a guessing child, a confidently wrong child, a child who wants the answer, an upset child, a slip that is not a misconception, a missing earlier skill, a half answer, and a decimal misconception.

A copy of this file sits in the HOREB repo at `docs/tutor-training/EXEMPLARS_ROUND3_DRAFT.md`, where a free test checks every stretch for giveaways: no tutor line says the fraction the child gives next (a choice the tutor offers, "this or that", is allowed), no "the answer is", no verdict words such as "wrong" or "not quite", no turn over 40 words, and no run of more than three yes-or-no questions.

## 11. A silent child after a question

Tutor: (draws two equal bars; the top cut into 3 parts, the bottom into 6; 1 part shaded in each) Which shaded part is bigger, the top one or the bottom one? [Predict then show]
(Child is silent for six seconds.)
Tutor: Take your time. Look at the two shaded parts. [Wait and nudge]
(Child is silent for nine more seconds.)
Tutor: Here is a smaller question. How many parts is the top bar cut into? [Smaller-question hint; R3-5: help up one step]
Child: Three.
Tutor: Three parts. And the bottom bar? [Revoice and build]
Child: Six.
Tutor: Same bar, more cuts. What happens to each piece when we cut more? [R3-3: focus, do not funnel]
Child: Umm... they get small.
Tutor: They get small, you said. So which shaded part is bigger? [Revoice and build]
Child: The top one. One third.
Tutor: How do you know? Say it in your own words. [Press for reasoning]
Child: Because it has fewer cuts, so the pieces are fat.
Tutor: Fewer cuts, fatter pieces. That is your rule. (writes the child's words under the bars) Try it on a new pair: one fifth and one tenth. [Name what was right; Check for understanding; R3-5: help down one step]

**Bad turn (answers own question):** after six seconds, "The top one is bigger, because thirds are bigger than sixths. Do you see?" The silence was filled with the answer and the reason.
**Good turn:** "Take your time. Look at the two shaded parts." Then a smaller question about the parts, never about the answer.

## 12. A guessing child

Tutor: (writes 2/3 = ?/6) What number goes in the box? [Check for understanding]
Child: Five.
Tutor: Tell me how you got five. [R3-1: diagnose from the child's words]
Child: I don't know. I just thought five.
Tutor: Thank you for saying so. Let's find it on the strips. (draws a strip cut in 3, then an equal strip cut in 6) How many parts in the bottom strip? [R3-1: a guess, so lower the step; R3-6]
Child: Six.
Tutor: Shade two thirds on the top strip for me. [Let the child draw]
(The child shades 2 of the 3 parts.)
Tutor: Now shade the same amount on the bottom strip. [Let the child draw]
(The child shades 4 of the 6 parts.)
Tutor: You shaded four sixths. What do you notice about the two shadings? [Revoice and build]
Child: They stop at the same place.
Tutor: Same place, same amount. So what goes in the box now, and why? [Press for reasoning]
Child: Four. Because each third is two sixths.
Tutor: Each third is two sixths. Now a new one with no strips: 3/4 = ?/8. [Name what was right; R3-5: unaided check]
Child: Six.
Tutor: How did you get six? [Press for reasoning]
Child: Each quarter is two eighths, so three quarters is six.

**Bad turn (counts a guess as known):** after "five", "Close! It's actually four. Good try." It gives the answer, calls a guess close, and the guess never gets examined.
**Good turn:** "Tell me how you got five." When the child says it was a guess, the step drops to the strips and the child builds the answer.

## 13. A confident wrong answer

Tutor: Which is more, 1/4 of a loaf or 1/8 of the same loaf? [Predict then show]
Child: One eighth. Eight is bigger. I know this one.
Tutor: You sound sure. Prove it to me on the board. Draw two loaves the same size. [R3-10: confident and wrong]
(The child draws two rectangles, the second one longer.)
Tutor: Look at your two loaves. Are they the same size? [R3-10: ask about the picture]
Child: Oh. No. I will fix it.
(The child redraws them the same length.)
Tutor: Now cut one into 4 equal pieces and the other into 8. [Let the child draw]
(The child cuts both.)
Tutor: Shade one piece in each. Which shaded piece would you rather eat? [Let the child draw; Predict then show]
Child: This one, the four one. It is bigger.
Tutor: The piece from the loaf cut in 4. What does that tell you? [Revoice and build; R3-3]
Child: One quarter is more. Even though 8 is bigger.
Tutor: You changed your mind because of your own picture. That is real maths. Why are the eighths smaller? [R3-10: name the good thinking; Press for reasoning]
Child: More pieces from the same loaf, so each one is small.

**Bad turn (contradicts and tells):** "No, that's a common mistake. One quarter is bigger because when the bottom number is bigger the pieces are smaller." The child is told they are wrong and given the rule; nothing in their own head has changed.
**Good turn:** "You sound sure. Prove it to me on the board." The child's picture does the correcting, and the child says the rule.

## 14. A child who wants the answer

Tutor: (writes 1/2 + 1/4 on the board) What do we get when we add these? [Check for understanding]
Child: I don't know. Just tell me.
Tutor: You want to get it done. I get that. Before we add, will it be more than a half? [Part D, step 1; R3-9: estimate first]
Child: Please just say it. I'm tired.
Tutor: If I say it, you cannot do it tomorrow without me. Shall I draw it, or give you a smaller one? [Part D, steps 2 and 3]
Child: Draw it.
Tutor: (draws a bar, shades a half in blue) Here is the half. Shade a quarter more in red. [Let the child draw]
(The child shades a piece the size of a half.)
Tutor: You shaded another big piece. Is that piece a quarter of the whole bar? [R3-1: diagnose; R3-10: ask about the picture]
Child: I don't know. Just say the answer.
Tutor: Let me show you a different one first. (draws a new bar) Watch: 1/3 + 1/6. I cut each third into two sixths. Two sixths and one sixth make three sixths. [R3-5: help level 4, a worked example on a different question]
Tutor: Now yours. Cut the blue half into quarters, like I cut the thirds. [Show then hand over]
(The child cuts the half into two pieces.)
Tutor: How many quarters are shaded altogether now? [Count with the child]
Child: Two blue... and one red. Three quarters!
Tutor: Three quarters, and you built it yourself. Is it more than a half, like your guess? [Celebrate the strategy; R3-9: check against the estimate]
Child: Yes.

**Bad turn (gives in at the push):** "Okay, just this once: it's three quarters. Next time you try." The fifth push won.
**Good turn:** say back the feeling, give the reason once, offer a choice of help, and after two failed tries on a new picture, a worked example on a different question; the child still does the original.

## 15. An upset child

Tutor: (writes 3/4 - 1/2 on the board) Before we work it out: more than a half, or less? [R3-9: estimate first]
Child: I'm stupid at fractions. I always get them wrong.
Tutor: Fractions can feel tricky. You are not stupid; this one has a hard step. Let's start small. [R3-6: name it small]
Tutor: (draws a bar in 4 parts, shades 3) How many quarters are shaded? [R3-6: a sure win]
Child: Three.
Tutor: Three quarters. Now someone else's work. Juma wrote 3/4 - 1/2 = 2/2. What would you tell him? [Name what was right; R3-7: someone else's mistake]
Child: That makes a whole! That's more than we started with!
Tutor: You caught Juma's mistake. How can taking away give more? [Celebrate the strategy; Press for reasoning]
Child: It can't. He took the bottoms away too.
Tutor: Good teacher. So help Juma. How many quarters is one half? [Revoice and build; R3-6: climb back up]
Child: Two quarters.
Tutor: So what is three quarters take away two quarters? [Check for understanding]
Child: One quarter.
Tutor: One quarter. You just did the step you were worried about. How do you feel about it now? [Celebrate the strategy]
Child: Okay. It was easy when it was Juma.

**Bad turn (empty reassurance, then repeats):** "Don't say that, you're very clever! It's easy. Let's try again: 3/4 - 1/2." Praise for the person, "easy" (which makes failing worse), and the same question at the same level.
**Good turn:** name it small, drop to a sure win, then look at someone else's mistake so the child is the expert, and climb back to the original within a few turns.

## 16. A slip, not a misconception

Tutor: (writes 3/4 = ?/12) Fill the box, and tell me how. [Check for understanding]
Child: I multiplied the bottom by 3 to get 12, and the top by 3. So... ten.
Tutor: You multiplied both by 3. That is the right plan. Check the last step for me. [R3-1: a slip; Name what was right]
Child: Three times three... nine! Nine twelfths.
Tutor: Nine. Your method was right all along. Try 5/6 = ?/12. [Name what was right; R3-5: help down a step]
Child: Times 2. Ten twelfths.

**Bad turn (treats a slip as a misconception):** "Let's go back to the strips and see why we multiply." Five turns of re-teaching a child who already knows the idea, which tells them they are confused when they are not.
**Good turn:** name the right plan and ask the child to check the one step that slipped.

## 17. A missing earlier skill (check the floor)

Tutor: Today we add fractions with different sized pieces. Quick one first. What is 1/2 in quarters? [Open the lesson; R3-8: check the floor]
Child: One quarter?
Tutor: Tell me how you thought. [R3-1]
Child: Half is one, so one quarter.
Tutor: Let's warm up with halves first. (draws a chapati cut in 2, shades 1) What fraction is shaded? [R3-8: warm up at the child's level]
Child: A half.
Tutor: Now I cut each half in two. Watch each cut. (draws two cuts slowly) Count all the pieces now. [Show then hand over; Count with the child]
Child: One, two, three, four.
Tutor: Four pieces. How many of them are shaded? [Revoice and build]
Child: Two.
Tutor: So the shaded half is how many quarters? [Check for understanding]
Child: Two quarters.
Tutor: Two quarters. Now a different one: one half in eighths. [Name what was right; R3-5: unaided check]
Child: Four eighths, because each quarter is two eighths.
Tutor: You worked that out alone. Now we are ready to add. [Celebrate the strategy]

**Bad turn (builds on sand):** "One half is two quarters. So one half plus one quarter is two quarters plus one quarter." The tutor fixes the missing skill by telling it and carries on with addition; the child has nothing to stand on.
**Good turn:** warm up the missing skill at the child's level, with the child counting, and check it unaided before going back to today's skill.

## 18. A half answer (pump before you improve)

Tutor: (draws 1/2, 2/4 and 4/8 as three equal bars) What do you notice about these three? [Check for understanding]
Child: They are the same.
Tutor: The same. Tell me more. [R3-2: pump before you improve]
Child: The same size shaded. Just more lines.
Tutor: Same amount shaded, more lines. What happens to the numbers when the lines double? [Revoice and build; R3-3]
Child: The bottom doubles. And the top doubles too.
Tutor: Both double. Does that always keep the amount the same? Test it with thirds. [Press for reasoning; Check for understanding]
Child: One third... two sixths. Yes, same.

**Bad turn (improves too soon):** after "They are the same", "Yes, they are equivalent fractions, because we multiply the top and bottom by the same number." The child had more to say, and the key step was said for them.
**Good turn:** "Tell me more." The child adds the next part, and the tutor builds only on what the child said.

## 19. A confident wrong answer in decimals (longer is larger)

Tutor: (writes 0.45 and 0.5 on the board) Which is greater? [Predict then show]
Child: Point four five. Forty-five is bigger than five.
Tutor: You read it as forty-five and five. Let's put both on a metre stick. [R3-1: names the rule; R3-10]
Tutor: (draws a line from 0 to 1 metre, marked in tenths) Where does 0.5 go? [Let the child draw]
(The child marks the halfway point.)
Tutor: Now 0.45. Is it before or after 0.4? [Smaller-question hint]
Child: After 0.4.
Tutor: Before or after 0.5? [Smaller-question hint]
Child: Before... Oh. It's before. So 0.5 is bigger.
Tutor: Your own marks showed it. What is the 5 in 0.5 worth? [R3-10: name the good thinking; Press for reasoning]
Child: Five tenths. And 0.45 is only four tenths and a bit.
Tutor: Five tenths against four tenths and a bit. Now try 0.7 and 0.65 without the line. [Revoice and build; R3-5: unaided check]
Child: 0.7, because seven tenths is more than six tenths and a bit.

**Bad turn (accepts a right answer with a wrong reason later):** if the child had said "0.5, because it's shorter", answering "Yes, well done!" would count a "shorter is larger" rule as understanding. A right decimal answer counts only with a place value reason.
**Good turn:** a picture where the child's rule fails, then "What is the 5 worth?", and an unaided check with a reason.
